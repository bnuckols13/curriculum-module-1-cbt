Curriculum · Module 1 · Adults · 45 min · 4–5 people

Module 1: CBT

The check-in collects the group's own examples in a few words each. Part 2 teaches the five-part model and the two paths through something hard, using one of those examples on the screen, then opens into discussion. Run it with the screen shared.

A few words, no details. The thought, not the whole story behind it. No numbers, no bodies, no foods.

Part 1 · Intro, check-in, getting ready

:00–:12

Arrive, then collect examples. Type each answer into its column on the screen as people say it. Passing is fine.

Check-in · :00–:03

Name, and one word for how you're arriving.

Avoiding

What's something you've been putting off lately?

What's something people tend to put off, even when part of them wants to do it?

A challenge

What's a challenge you've set for yourself, big or small?

What's something you're trying to get better at?

Something hard

What's been hard lately? Just name it.

What makes a week feel hard for people?

Follow-ups if an answer is thin: "What makes that one hard to start?" "What would it mean to you to do it?" "How long has that been around?" Keep the answer to a few words on the screen.

Part 2 · Inquiry and engagement

:12–:35

About thirteen minutes of teaching with the screen, then ten of discussion.

Teach · one example, five parts · :12–:16

Click one of the group's answers in Part 1, or pick a category. It loads into the chain.

Ask the room for each box and type over the defaults: "What might the first thought be? What would someone feel? Where would they feel it? What might they do?" Name the automatic thought: fast, on its own, feels like a fact, often a prediction.

Teach · the five-part model · :16–:20

Click each part. The definition shows with the group's example underneath.

Point: each part affects the others, so a change in any one can shift the rest.

Teach · two ways it can go · :20–:25

Show "Move away," then "Stay with it," then "Compare."

Point: moving away brings relief now and a stronger thought next time. Staying with it is harder now, and it's how people learn they can handle it. That practice, done on purpose and in small steps, is exposure.

Discuss · :25–:35

Which part usually shows up first for you: a thought, a feeling, or your body?

What's a first thought that tends to show up when you think about something you've been putting off?

What does moving away look like for people? What does staying with it look like?

What helps a person stay with something hard, even for a little longer?

Put examples on the board as people talk, in their words, and read each row back.

Say more.Anyone else?What did you notice in your body?What happened next?What did that thought predict?What would a small step look like?

Part 3 · Next steps and takeaways

:35–:45

Go around, then the practice for the week, then read back the board.

  • What's one idea from today you want to remember?
  • What's one small step toward something you named today?
  • For this week: catch one moment. The situation, the first thought, the feeling, and whether you moved away or stayed with it. The full thought record is there for anyone who wants more columns.
  • One word for what you're leaving with.

Notes

  • Keep the teaching short. Click, ask the room, say the point once, move on.
  • Use the group's examples over the defaults whenever you can.
  • If an answer names food, exercise, or a body goal, thank them and pick a different example for the screen.
  • Small steps are chosen with the treatment team. Don't assign one in group.
  • If "I feel fat" comes up as a feeling, ask once what feeling might be underneath it, and move on.
  • If someone starts into a big story, thank them and ask them to bring it to their therapist this week.
  • If someone says something like "everyone would be better off without me," check in with them directly right away under the program's risk protocol and route to their therapist the same day.
